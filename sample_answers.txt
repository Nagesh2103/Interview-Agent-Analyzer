I've worked mainly with Tableau and Power BI, and some matplotlib/seaborn in Python for quicker exploratory work. For choosing a chart type, I think about what relationship I'm trying to show: trends over time go on a line chart, comparisons across categories go on a bar chart, distributions go on a histogram or box plot, and relationships between two numeric variables go on a scatter plot. I also try to keep it simple, since a chart that needs a paragraph to explain usually means the wrong chart type was picked.
It depends on why the data is missing and how much of it is missing. If it's a small amount missing completely at random, I might use mean, median, or mode imputation, or a more model-based approach like k-nearest neighbors for numeric fields. If a large chunk of a column is missing or the missingness itself seems meaningful, I'd rather flag it with a missing indicator column or drop the column entirely than impute something misleading. I always check how the choice affects downstream analysis before committing to it.
I start by understanding the business question behind the dataset, then do an initial profile: row counts, column types, missing values, and summary statistics. From there I look for obvious data quality issues before doing any real analysis. Once the data looks clean, I explore distributions and relationships between variables, usually visually first, then formalize anything interesting with actual numbers. I try to end with a small number of clear, actionable conclusions rather than a long list of observations.
I'd start by joining the demographics, purchase history, and transactional tables into an analysis-ready table, probably at the customer level. Then I'd segment customers by behavior, like purchase frequency, average order value, and recency, to find distinct groups such as high-value repeat buyers versus one-time purchasers. I'd look at which demographic factors correlate with those segments and which product categories drive repeat purchases. The goal would be actionable insights like which segments to target for retention campaigns versus which ones need a different acquisition strategy.
I once had to tell a marketing team that a campaign they were proud of hadn't actually moved the needle on sales. Instead of leading with statistics, I started with the business question they cared about, showed a simple before-and-after chart, and used a plain-language analogy to explain why the change wasn't statistically meaningful. I avoided jargon like p-values and instead focused on what the number meant for their decision, and I made sure to leave time for questions so they could push back on my interpretation before we finalized anything.
